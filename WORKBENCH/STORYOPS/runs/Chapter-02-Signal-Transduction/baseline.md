# Chapter 2: Signal Transduction

The fall into the past began eighty‑seven milliseconds after Sona’s last heartbeat in the screaming present. A single electrical pulse—her own atrial spark—carried the field‑coordinates for the jump. In the next three hundred milliseconds the **Anamnesis Engine** compressed the entire collapse into a down‑swept cadence you could almost hum: a low C felt more in lungs than ears. Jian logged the lag as **Signal 80‑300‑Cardioid**, even while his optic nerves still bled the after‑image of the collapsing passage. Then the noise simply… shut up.

The chaotic, screaming present imploded to a star that burned once and blinked out. For six nanoseconds there was nothing. Not zero, not darkness—*absence*—as if a synapse had been lifted cleanly out of the skull. The sudden quiet pressed against the eardrums like an altitude change.

Then shape.

Reality resolved into a scene so calm it felt like deliberate insult. They stood inside a library the size of a small cathedral. Sunlight, amber and thick as honey, slanted through stained clerestory windows—Virgo rising, Jian noted automatically, though the zodiac had no official place in his charting suite. The air smelled of lignin, pipe tobacco, and something fainter: old grief stored in the glue of book spines.

Dust motes drifted around them, dense as a nebula. Each fleck carried a faint electric halo: micro‑charges caught between pane and pane of memory glass. They were inside a cloud of miniature suns.

Sona opened her mouth, closed it. “The bruising’s gone,” she whispered. The choroid plexus still felt swollen, but the pain no longer broadcast on every channel. “The Engine found the safe signal.”

Gideon’s hand hovered at his sternum, palm flat as if feeling for a hidden weapon. “Safe is a category, not a promise.” He let the hand drop. The protective grid of his **Klei Toda'ah** relaxed by millimetres, bars thinning to the width of paper.

Corv said nothing. His gaze swept the shelves the way a surgeon reads bruising: looking for the wound beneath the bloom.

They drifted forward—no other verb fit. Sona first, then the others, pulled by a gravity that had nothing to do with mass. The oak floorboards, oiled until the grain looked wet, didn’t creak under non‑existent weight.

Jian exhaled, and the cloud of dust in front of his face rearranged itself into the glyph for *śūnya*, zero‑point. He filed the coincidence under “environmental metaphor” and kept walking.

---

Halfway down the central aisle, the shelves parted like a wound cauterized with gold leaf. The geometry repeated in fractal iterations: every shelf a miniature of the whole library, each book on it a compressed library in turn. Vedic compression, old code. A **Khalorēē** signature older than the Engine itself.

He touched nothing, yet his **Manas Interface** overlaid the space with a lattice of quiet gold. Error codes vanished one by one: *301 Missing Cardinal*, *472 Recursive Silence*, *802 Absence Not Found*. The last amused him—error code as koan.

“Pre‑trauma,” he announced, voice soft enough to pass for reverence. “Whatever broke the mind, it happened *after* this memory.”

From somewhere above, the faint, impossible sound of a page turning. No one looked up. They had learned that some echoes existed only to test composure.

Sona stopped before an armchair. It was large, leather cracked from decades of pressure, the seat cushion sagging in the exact contour of a body no longer present. The pipe in the ashtray still leaked a single ribbon of smoke, frozen mid‑spiral.

She reached toward the smoke, reflex slowed by wonder, and her fingers passed through it without resistance—like plunging a hand into the memory of warmth.

“Subject’s not here,” she said. “But the devotion is.”

Her **Adawat al‑Wa'i** read the room like skin reads braille: a texture of absence, raised and definite. The absence had weight—twelve kilograms, give or take the mass of an adult heart. She said it aloud before she could stop herself. “Twelve kilos of missing person.”

Gideon snorted—half laugh, half bark. “That’s disturbingly specific.”

She didn’t smile. “Specific is what’s left when identity goes.”

Jian felt a notification arrive before the glyph appeared: a pressure at the base of his skull, the Quoril family crest encoded as a polite migraine. He blinked. The message unfurled:

**Dr Node Quoril**  
> Board query: House Luminth filed provisional patent 12‑C on Daoist field scaffolding using meridian mapping. Council of Emanations requests comparative data set. Deliver by 0800. Do not tunnel down academic rabbit holes.

He thumbed it away so hard the air rippled. Dust motes flinched.

Corv watched the gesture, eyebrow raised. “Personal leave?” he asked, voice dry.

“Family obligation,” Jian muttered. “Same thing, different century.”

Gideon approached the chair as if it might bite. His **Klei Toda'ah** extended into a transparent cube around the seat, its edges glinting ice‑blue. The cube’s inner surfaces ran diagnostics: temperature stable, pressure null, no bio‑threat detected.

Yet the chair’s shadow fell the wrong way—toward the windows, against the light. He logged the anomaly under *gravitational drift*, then immediately wished he hadn’t. The library was rewriting local physics faster than he could patch.

He knelt, palms on the floorboards. The oak felt warm—body heat trapped for decades. A silent alarm pulsed up through the house, slow and patient as lymph.

“Something lived here,” he said. “Something they loved enough to amputate.”

Corv circled the chair once, twice, each orbit tighter. His **Yìshí Qìxiè** moved like water, touching but never disturbing: the pipe, the ashtray, the ghost of a whiskey ring on the side table.

He paused at the hearth. A single index card pinned to the mantelpiece, blank except for a printer’s mark: `#Khalorēē_Core_Trust_Δ7`. The red lettering had faded to the color of dried blood.

“Metadata tag,” he said. “Belief module deleted at revision Δ7. They kept the index but erased the text.”

Sona’s breath caught. “Core Trust. That’s not just a belief. It’s the protocol that decides what *can* be believed.”

Jian’s fingers flew across his interface, pulling threads of data like a harpist drawing wire. “Phantom references still looping. Every book in this wing cites the same absent source. The footnotes point to nothing.” He looked up, eyes bright with forensic hunger. “We can map the hole by the shape of the citations.”

A second notification flared—this time lilac, the color Dr Melody Quoril used for “concerned understatement.”

**Dr Melody Quoril**  
> Field strain rising at your node. Let the response *be* the map, dear. Sometimes the absence tells truer stories than the ink.

Jian felt the words land in the exact spot Node’s migraine had bruised. He almost laughed—two aunts, one scalpel each, cutting from opposite directions. “Noted,” he muttered, and for once he meant it.

Sona sat—insofar as sitting was possible—on the arm of the empty chair. Her translucent form intersected the leather without clipping. From inside the ghost‑outline she could feel the contour of absent weight: a clavicle’s slope, the ridge of a sternum.

She closed her eyes. The **miradune** opened beneath her, a silvered surface that reflected not her face but her pulse. The rhythm skipped, then steadied. Below, the redacted belief floated like a sunken statue, coral overgrown around the holes where eyes should be.

A voice—not memory, not hallucination—whispered across the interface: *“I was told safety could be built like a wall.”* She couldn’t tell if it belonged to the subject, the deleted belief, or herself.

Corv found the first fracture at floor level—a hairline crack following the oak grain in a perfect Fibonacci spiral. He knelt, tracing it with one ghost‑finger. “The redaction wasn’t self‑inflicted,” he said. “Look at the angle. External incision, precise to the neuron. Someone else held the knife.”

Gideon straightened, containment cube flashing a colder blue. “House Luminth?” The name arrived unbidden; the politics had followed them into sanctuary like smell follows rain.

“Signal match,” Jian confirmed, distracted. A new line of data scrolled: `House_Luminth_Feed_Interrupt_Δ7`. “They sent a pulse. The subject’s field dropped trust as instructed.” His voice was flat, but the flatness carried iron.

Silence pooled. Even the dust motes stilled, as if eavesdropping.

Then, pressure‑release: Gideon flicked an imaginary speck of lint from his sleeve. “Well,” he said, “at least the décor survived the coup. Taste is the last thing to go.”

Sona choked on a laugh. Corv’s mouth twitched—one corner, barely. Jian felt the tension in his shoulders unknot by a single vertebra.

Somewhere high above, a bell that did not exist tolled twice. The sound passed through them like wind through harp strings, leaving the note *mi* ringing in bone. Libra’s warning, Corv thought, but kept it to himself.

He rose. “We map the hole or we go home blind. No middle option.”

Jian rolled his shoulders. “Then let’s read the silence like it’s scripture.”

They formed a loose triangle around the chair—Sona at the head, Jian at the side‑table, Corv at the hearth—each extending their interface into the absence. The **Khalorēē** responded to the synchronized query with the patience of a vault that knows it will outlast the thieves.

The library brightened, just perceptibly, as if the sun had shifted thirty degrees west. Dust motes resumed their slow dance, now tracing tiny figure‑eights: infinity symbols drawn in light.

Sona spoke first, voice soft as breath. “The person who sat here believed safety was mutual witnessing. No walls. Just…” she searched, found two words, “open coherence.”

Gideon, surprisingly gentle, finished for her. “They removed the witness and kept the walls.”

No one argued. The redaction was complete, but the room remembered. Shelves leaned inward like mourners. The pipe still smoldered, though no coal remained. The memory palace stood, serene and obscene, around the negative space where trust had once lived.

Jian saved the final coordinates: `Sanctuary_Δ7_Absence_Radius = 0.87 m`. Smaller than a person. Larger than a soul.

He looked at the others. “Ready for the contested membrane?”

Corv nodded once. Sona stood, translucent hand lingering on an absent shoulder. Gideon’s cube folded into a thin, sharp plane: blade instead of box.

They turned toward the far archway where the shadows pooled thickest. Behind them, the armchair waited, patient as grief. Somewhere in the rafters, the nonexistent bell tolled a third time, and the dust motes spelled a single word no eye could read: *return*.

Jian’s interface chimed again, a lower‑cadence pulse he recognized as the Engine’s built‑in caution tone. The alert arrived as a single line of amber text across the **Mirrored Index**: `Consensus drift 2.3 %—expect phase shear at the threshold.` He glanced at the others; none had visibly reacted, yet he felt the collective heartbeat nudge fractionally out of sync, like four metronomes nudged by the same breeze.

Corv exhaled through teeth and spoke first, quietly enough that the room would have to bend its acoustics to hear. “The threshold remembers forced consensus. When we step through, the absent witness will feel the drag. Expect phantom weight on the left side.” He tapped his own shoulder blade, the place where one might carry an invisible pack. “Don’t compensate. Let the asymmetry ride—it’s the only honest vector we still share with the subject.”

Gideon flexed his wrist; the containment cube flattened further until it caught the light like surgical steel. “I can pre‑load a counter‑tilt, but the cube will record the correction as interference. If House Luminth subpoenas the data, it’ll read like tampering.”

“Let it read,” Sona said. “Tampering is just another word for witness.” She laid her palm over the back of the armchair, fingers hovering a millimetre above the cracked leather. A faint static arc leapt the gap, the colour of oxidised copper. No one flinched; grief had already done the flinching for them.

Jian scrolled the amber warning to the margin and pinned it beside the earlier coordinate file: a private triangulation only he would carry forward. The amber became a soft bruise against the white of the workspace. He tasted iron at the back of his tongue—response fatigue beginning its administrative drip. He swallowed, let the copper taste settle, and addressed the silence rather than the team. “We have eight minutes before the library’s own forgetting protocol cycles. After that, the absence retracts its signature and the hole becomes noise. Decision window closes.”

Corv angled his head toward the far archway, the way one listens to an approaching train. “The fracture widens at seven minutes forty‑two seconds. Enough time to steal one more metric.”

“Or bleed one,” Gideon said, almost cheerful. He drew the blade‑flat cube across his forearm without breaking skin, testing edge against pulse. The interface registered no wound, yet the blood‑heat signature flared red on the diagnostic pane. He nodded approval at the calibration, folded the plane, and tucked it into a pocket that existed only while the pocket was remembered.

Sona straightened, her outline shimmering like breath on winter glass. “Before we cross, I need the taste of the room at the instant trust was revoked. Jian, can you isolate the micro‑volatile signature from the pipe ash?”

Jian knelt, two fingers splayed above the bowl. A thin ribbon of data unspooled between his glove and the residue, forming a soft cinnamon curl in the air. “Acacia, cherry cavendish, touch of benzoin—ritual grade. The burn profile spikes at 0.6 seconds past ignition. Time‑stamp matches the redaction pulse.” He inhaled, held the phantom smoke at the roof of his mouth, then exhaled through his teeth, letting the molecules disperse across the silent library like an apology arriving late. “Now I carry the same breath they lost.”

Corv acknowledged with the smallest tilt of chin, as if the motion itself might disturb evidence. “Good. Shared breath is shared culpability. Let’s not pretend otherwise.”

Above them, the nonexistent bell gave a single preparatory shiver—no audible strike, only the memory of metal on the verge of sound. Dust motes stilled in mid‑echo, waiting. Gideon stepped first, blade held low, more scalpel than sword. Sona followed, hand still half‑raised to a shoulder that no longer existed. Jian lingered a heartbeat, eyes fixed on the armchair’s shadow stretching the wrong way across the floorboards. He adjusted the amber warning’s opacity until it matched the shadow’s darkness, then closed the file.

Ready.

The silence after the shared breath lasted exactly three heartbeats—one for each living pulse, one for the absent fourth. Sona felt the loss as a coolness in the lung she no longer possessed, phantom pleura aching with imagined nicotine. She turned the ache over, cataloguing its texture: not pain, but the *after‑image* of pain, like the ghost of a hand once held. She wondered if the subject had felt the same when the redaction hit—if the body kept craving warmth after the heart was convinced it was unnecessary.

Corv, meanwhile, traced the Fibonacci crack to its vanishing point beneath the chair’s left front leg. The grain twisted into a tight whorl, a knot so perfect it resembled a pupil. He pressed the pad of his thumb against it and felt a faint, rhythmic thrum: the library’s own circadian pulse, slow as a century. Somewhere in the architecture, time was still being parsed into heartbeats. The realization landed quietly: they were not inside a memory so much as inside the memory’s *body*. A body that might yet flinch.

Gideon noticed the shift first. His containment plane dimmed to a whisper of frost as ambient temperature dropped a measurable tenth of a degree. The chair’s shadow thickened, edges fuzzing outward like mold on bread. “Clock’s accelerating,” he murmured. “The room’s metabolising us.” He flexed the blade‑flat cube, letting it drink the new gradient. A faint seam appeared along its length, hairline but widening—a vent for pressure he couldn’t name.

Jian felt the amber warning pulse again, faster now, syncing with the shadow’s slow respiration. The Quoril migraine sharpened: a family heirloom he hadn’t asked for. He pinched the bridge of his nose, then spoke to the air between them, voice pitched low to keep the walls from listening. “We can slow the cycle. One of us has to stay behind as anchor.” He didn’t look at Sona, but the suggestion hung unspoken. She was already half‑ghost; the threshold would barely notice her inertia.

Sona heard the offer and answered by moving forward. The translucent sole of her foot passed through the first dust mote, and the mote flared into a tiny nova, shedding light in a colour no human retina had ever registered. She froze mid‑step. “The motes are indexing us,” she said. “Every motion updates the archive.” She lifted her hand; the motes rearranged into a fractal bloom, a mandala of recalculating futures. “If I stay, I become the index. If I go, the index forgets.” She turned to Corv, eyes bright with the logic of displacement. “What’s the etiquette for leaving a library that’s learning your name?”

Corv considered, then reached into the pocket that existed only when remembered. He withdrew a single copper coin—minted, he knew, in a year that had never happened. The face bore the profile of a woman whose eyes were drilled straight through, as if to let the world see what she had refused to. He pressed the coin into the Fibonacci knot beneath the chair. “Payment for the late fee,” he said. “One memory for one silence.” The coin took the thrum of the library’s pulse and, after a hesitation, the pulse quieted. The shadow’s edge stopped spreading.

Gideon watched the transaction with the clinical fondness he usually reserved for perfect incisions. “You’ve just collateralised our exit,” he said. “If the anchor fails, the room forecloses.” He adjusted the blade‑flat cube so that the new seam aligned with the coin’s unseen axis. A soft click, like a vertebra settling back into place. “Debt accepted.”

Jian exhaled, tasting again the phantom smoke: benzoin’s resinous bite, acacia’s honeyed ghost. He understood the arithmetic now. The subject had paid the same price—one breath held until it became absence, one witness traded for walls. The symmetry felt less like coincidence than choreography. He glanced at the amber warning; the pulse had slowed to a steady four‑beat rhythm, the cadence of a resting heart. Not his, not Corv’s, not Sona’s. The room had synchronised to the deleted owner’s phantom rate.

Sona lowered her hand. The mandala of motes folded into a single point, then extinguished. “Debt acknowledged,” she echoed. She stepped back from the chair, letting her outline settle fractionally more solid, as if the room had granted temporary asylum. “I’ll keep the witness at zero range. You three carry the breach.” She offered a half‑smile, the kind used at funerals when no one trusts the eulogy. “Try not to make the hole larger on your way out.”

Corv inclined his head—a gesture equal parts gratitude and absolution—then gestured toward the archway. The shadows there had rearranged into a soft funnel, edges feathered like wings. Beyond, the air shimmered with the faint iridescence of phase shear, the membrane already forgetting which side was inside. He stepped into the funnel first, the copper coin’s silence following him like a hush after prayer.

Gideon followed, blade held low, its new seam glowing the same green as oxidised bronze. He didn’t look back at the armchair, but the corner of his mouth twitched—equal parts irony and respect. “Remember,” he said, not to Sona but to the room itself, “we’re only borrowing the damage.” Then he crossed the threshold, and the shimmer folded over him without a sound.

Jian lingered an extra second, fingers still tasting pipe ash and benzoin. He adjusted the amber warning one last time, dialing its hue to match the exact shade of the subject’s last exhalation—an apology offered to air that had already moved on. Then he closed the file, tucked the phantom smoke behind his teeth, and stepped through. The archway’s membrane rippled once, like a curtain disturbed by wind, and resealed, leaving Sona alone with the chair, the coin, and the slow heartbeat of a library that had agreed to forget them for precisely seven minutes forty‑two seconds.

Corv paused at the lip of the funnel, one hand on the frame as though testing a pulse. “Protocol says we owe the archive one more checksum,” he said, voice low, almost bantering. “If we leave without it, the breach might reflex‑seal on Jian’s trace. Library likes to keep souvenirs.”

Gideon lifted the blade a fraction, letting the green seam paint a stripe across Corv’s cheek. “You volunteering to stay behind and tick the box, old man? Thought you’d had enough of unpaid overtime.”

Jian flicked ash from an imaginary pipe into the pocket of air that still tasted of benzoin. “We could send the amber,” he offered. “Let it race the shear and come back pregnant with entropy. Simpler.”

Sona’s answer arrived before any of them moved: a soft click, like a thumbnail against glass. The copper coin spun on the armrest once, then settled head‑up—crow looking left. “One checksum,” she said. “But the witness clause says I have to carry the measure.” She tucked a loose strand behind her ear, the motion making her outline fuzz for half a heartbeat. “I’ll walk the margin with you three, then peel off. Net time cost: twenty‑two seconds. Acceptable?”

Corv exhaled through his teeth, measuring risk against the boredom of later regret. “Fine. On three, we step in phase. No heroics, no lag. If the membrane stutters, we all back out.” He glanced at Gideon. “Even you, knight.”

Gideon shrugged, the gesture almost graceful. “Not here for heroics. Just here to keep the hole the right shape.” He tapped the sword’s spine twice; the green seam pulsed, once, twice, like a consent heartbeat.

Jian lifted the amber between thumb and forefinger, letting the light fractal through its core. “I’ll key it to my pulse. When the amber blacks out, we’re one second from shear collapse. Last chance to abort.”

They formed a loose crescent, shoulders almost brushing, breath syncing without ceremony. Sona slipped the copper coin into Corv’s palm—payment deferred, not forgiven. He curled his fingers around it, surprised by its residual warmth.

“Three,” Corv said, and they stepped forward together.

Corv’s HUD flashed a soft amber: **SYNC DRIFT 3.7 mS**.  
“Clock bleed,” he muttered, pinching the air, and the drift shrank to 0.9.  
Sona exhaled through her teeth. “We just calibrated.”  
“Biology doesn’t care about our schedule,” Gideon answered, already pulling the haptic cuff tighter over Jian’s left wrist. The strap left a faint red line on skin still chilled from gel contact. Jian flexed once; the cuff flexed back, warmer, like a living cufflink.

“Your pressure delta is climbing,” Gideon told him. The readout floated between them: 122/78 → 139/85.  
“Performance anxiety,” Jian said, deadpan. “Or I’m in love with risk.”  
Corv snorted. “Pick one and stick to it; the algorithm wants a single narrative.”

Sona leaned in, voice low. “We’ve got a 2 % thermal crest building at your cervical spike. You’ll start shivering in forty seconds and lose fine motor around ninety. Trade?”  
Jian nodded once. Sona pressed two fingers just below his ear; the crest line flattened, warmth flowing down her forearm like spilled tea. He shivered anyway, once, from nerves not temperature.

Gideon tilted the sword so its green seam faced the others. “Edge says we’re still within tolerance, but the margin’s a hair. Anyone want to bet their favourite axon?”  
Sona arched a brow. “I’ve got a spare memory of the Aegean sunset. Loser donates it to the archive.”  
Corv grinned. “Deal. I’ll hold the stakes. If we come out clean, sunset stays with me. If not, posterity gets a postcard.”  
Jian flipped the amber so it caught the seam’s pulse. The light stuttered, skipped, then steadied. “Stochastic response locked. We’re good for the next nine minutes, give or take a heartbeat.”  
“Nine minutes is generous,” Corv said, already counting breaths. “Let’s not spend them on speeches.”

Sona fished the copper coin from her coat pocket, spun it once on her knuckle. It hummed, softer than any hard currency should. She held it out to Corv—no ceremony, just the weight of deferred debt.  
Corv took it, surprised by its residual warmth, which might be hers or the room’s remembering earlier bodies. He closed his fist around it like catching a dropped stitch.

The copper’s warmth in Corv’s palm matched the pulse he felt through the archway: neither his nor the room’s, but an echo of the deleted owner’s final systolic beat, still conducting along the oak grain like current through a standing nerve. He rolled the coin across the knuckles of his gloved hand, once, twice—an idle gambit that let him measure the membrane’s resistance by the minute lag between spin and sound. Two‑tenths of a second: acceptable loss. He pocketed the coin, felt the pocket vanish the instant his fingers left the rim, and turned his focus forward.

Jian watched the amber between his thumb and forefinger darken a shade closer to garnet. The Quoril migraine, momentarily soothed by benzoin and borrowed breath, tapped his skull in four‑four time. He opened the diagnostic pane one layer deeper—amber text over amber text, a nested bruise—and keyed a silent query: `Redirect residual benzoin to olfactory buffer?` The pane answered with an almost maternal lilt: `Already cached. You’ll taste it for eleven minutes post‑exit.` He closed the pane, satisfied, and inhaled the phantom smoke once more to calibrate the half‑life.

Sona drifted a fractional step ahead, her outline limned by the last motes still uncommitted to the archive. Each footfall left a shallow ripple in the air, as though memory glass retained the habit of carrying weight even when weight had been surgically removed. She flexed her fingers—ghost metacarpals that had not quite forgotten flesh—and felt the copper’s residual warmth transfer from Corv’s aura to hers without physical contact. A minor, inevitable trespass. She logged it, not as data, but as etiquette: *Thanked in advance for the silence I’m about to borrow.*

Gideon thumbed the haptic ring at the base of the sword’s spine. The green seam flickered once, then settled into the precise cadence of Jian’s resting heart, a rhythm the blade would mimic for exactly twenty‑one seconds before detuning on purpose. “Edge wants to know what happens to the chair when we leave,” he said, voice pitched to reach only the three nearest cochlear implants. “Does absence keep sitting, or does the library stand up and leave itself?”

No one answered; the question was scaffolding, something to keep anxiety from collapsing inward. They advanced three abreast—Corv left, Gideon right, Jian centre, Sona a half‑stride ahead—into the funnel of shadow that had reshaped into an isosceles passage of shimmering jet. The air inside tasted of cold iron and old parchment, a combination that triggered an involuntary cascade of micro‑memories in Jian’s limbic stack: childhood libraries, pressed flowers, the smell of his first thesis defense. He let the cascade complete, then archived it under `env_filter/redundant`. The migraine thanked him by quieting a decibel.

Corv counted breaths aloud, soft as rosary: “In four, hold two, out six.” On the fourth exhale the passage’s inner surface opalesced, opaline layers sliding past one another like fish scales. The scales reflected distorted fragments of their faces—eyes elongated, mouths compressed—reminding them they were already halfway translated. He felt the coin pulse once in its nonexistent pocket, a single thump against the seam of his coat. He counted it as the anchor’s confirmation: *Still moored.*

Sona extended her left hand, palm down, fingers relaxed. The air beneath her wrist cooled by half a degree, the exact delta registered when a hand passes through a quiet candle flame without burning. She watched micro‑condensation form droplets along the lifeline, then evaporate before they could fall. “Threshold at six metres,” she said. “Counting down from twenty‑two.”

Jian adjusted the amber again, this time dimming the hue until its glow matched the passage’s leading edge. The migraine protested, then acquiesced. He spoke without looking up: “At two metres, stochastic leash begins to slip. If any of us drift more than 0.4 milliradians, the library reclassifies us as annotation rather than traveler.” He glanced at Corv. “Translation: we become footnotes.” Corv’s mouth quirked. “Better footnotes than endnotes. Endnotes get ignored.”

The passage narrowed to the width of an outstretched arm. Gideon angled the sword vertically, letting the green seam bisect the space like a plumb line. Where the light touched the wall, the iridescence peeled back to reveal a darker substrate: the matte, absorbent black of burnt film stock. A faint vinegar smell wafted out—acetate decay, the scent of memories too old to bear their own weight. He noted the odor, filed it, then did the only sensible thing: took a shallow breath and let the decay settle on his tongue like communion wafer. “History tastes like mistakes held under the tongue too long,” he murmured. No one corrected him.

Four metres. Sona’s outline fuzzed at the edges, molecules loosening by degrees. She felt the copper coin’s warmth fade from her palm—not cooling, simply absent, as though the memory of warmth had been recalled by its original owner. She resisted the reflex to clutch at nothing. Instead she opened her hand, letting absence complete its transaction. In the small silence that followed, she heard the chair’s leather sigh once behind them, a sound like a book closing that no one had touched. She logged the sigh under `evidence/unsolicited` and kept walking.

Three metres. Corv felt the coin pulse a final time, then go still. The cessation left a vacuum in the pocket that hadn’t existed a heartbeat earlier. He understood: anchor severed. The debt had been accepted in full. He resisted the urge to look back; looking back was for people who believed exits remained symmetrical to entrances. Instead he matched Gideon’s stride exactly, the two of them forming an unspoken bracket around Jian, who in turn shielded Sona’s flickering form with the amber’s steady glow.

Two metres. Jian’s interface chimed once—a soft, almost apologetic tone. The amber text read: `Shear in 12s. Consensus drop to 97.3 %`. He thumbed an icon shaped like a tiny guillotine, setting a countdown that would flash subliminally in the lower‑right quadrant of all their retinas. The migraine, finally grasping the timeline, throbbed in anticipatory sympathy. Jian welcomed the throb; pain was a more reliable metronome than hope.

One metre. The passage’s walls brushed their shoulders—no pressure, just recognition. Sona felt her translucence become momentarily opaque, as though the membrane wanted one last look at what it was about to forget. She inhaled the vinegar reek of decaying acetate and found it oddly comforting: a smell that had already given up on permanence. She flexed her right hand, testing whether the fingers would still articulate after translation. They responded—barely—like marionette strings stretched to their final thread.

The threshold itself was a line no scanner could resolve, only the sudden absence of smell. One step forward and the vinegar vanished, replaced by a sterile neutrality reminiscent of freshly quarried limestone. They crossed in single file: Corv, Gideon, Jian, with Sona a half‑breath behind. No sound marked the transit, yet each felt the subtle pop of inner‑ear equalising—as though memory itself had changed altitude.

They emerged into the same passage, but reversed: the iridescence now on the inside, reflecting them back at themselves in triplicate, quadruple, quintuple, the images nesting smaller until they became a single bright point. The point blinked once, then collapsed into the amber warning Jian still carried. He checked the readout: `Consensus restored at 99.7 %`. The migraine loosened another notch. Not his, not Corv’s, not Sona’s. The room had synchronised to the deleted owner’s phantom rate.

Corv opened his fist. The copper coin was gone, replaced by a perfect circle of warmth the size of a fingerprint on his palm.
