# Chapter 16: The Wilt

---

The Myocardial Chorus had learned to sing again.

That was what the telemetry promised.

Six weeks earlier the team had left their last descent with no artifact, no coronation, only a harder discipline: distinct witnesses, shared field, disciplined exchange. Mira Verath's line on Tessari had become the first case strong enough to suggest that discipline might survive outside the chamber itself. If the recovery failed now, the failure would not stay private. The review Houses would treat it as proof that coherence could be induced inside a chamber and repossessed the moment ordinary life resumed.

Jian stood at his station in the Anamnesis Engine chamber, his Manas Interface, the Vedic witness vessel, painting the air before him with luminous threads of data. The harmonics that had once been jagged with inherited fear, those serrated waveforms that had cut through three generations of the Verath lineage, now held a sustained, breathable coherence. The healed passages glowed with the soft iridescence of Coherence Cultivation: stabilized belief systems, integrated trauma residue, liberated emotional pathways.

The earliest wound on record belonged to Anvel Verath, the first of the line to make law out of injury. Three generations later, the law still ran beneath every healed passage like old plumbing under new tile. Jian had mapped it. He had not been able to remove it.

The numbers held a stark, architectural beauty. The mathematics of healing had resolved into elegant symmetry.

And yet.

Jian watched the readouts with a mathematician's superstitious dread: not the fear of being wrong, but the fear of being right for the wrong reason. His Type Five Khalorēē had been trained by a lifetime of pattern recognition to trust data absolutely, but it had also learned, through three years of working with the Somanaut team, that the most dangerous anomalies announced themselves in whispers.

The curve was changing.

At first it was subtle. A single overtone misaligned by a fraction of a hertz, like a choir member swallowing grief mid-phrase. Jian flagged it with a gesture, pulling the waveform into magnification. The Manas Interface responded with surgical precision, isolating the aberrant cadence and running comparative analysis against the baseline measurements from seven days prior.

*Variance: 0.03 Hz. Within acceptable parameters.*

Jian frowned. He ran the diagnostic again.

The same result returned, wrapped in the comfortable language of technical normalcy. But his neuro-cartographer's instincts, those finely trained hunches that had mapped the consciousness architecture of forty-seven subjects across three galaxies, refused to be soothed.

He blamed the diagnostic array. He recalibrated.

The anomaly persisted.

He blamed the ambient noise of the station, the subtle tremor of the life support systems, the electromagnetic whisper of the Anamnesis Engine's probability wave generators. He filtered for interference.

It moved through the Chorus the way mold moves through a loaf: not a dramatic invasion, but a slow reclaiming of what had been temporarily held in light.

Sona registered it before the machine admitted it.

She was seated in the listening alcove, her eyes closed, her Adawat al-Wa'i, the Sufi witness vessel she had inherited through her line, extended outward in a web of empathic perception. Unlike Jian's analytical instruments, her vessel did not translate the data into numbers first. It translated it into texture, temperature, taste.

The healed song had had a particular feel: warm palms pressed over a bruised ribcage, breath returning to a clenched throat, the first morning after a fever breaks. For weeks now, whenever she had reached out to sense the Verath lineage's Chorus, that warmth had greeted her. The integration had been real. The healing had held.

Now that feel was thinning.

Sona's breath caught. She opened her eyes.

"It's not breaking," she said quietly, her voice carrying across the chamber to where Jian stood frozen at his console. "It's drying."

The word surprised her as it left her mouth. She had not planned to say drying. But her Khalorēē, that ancient system of relational perception that metabolized reality as symphony, had found the precise metaphor. The Chorus was not being attacked. It was being desiccated. The moisture of healing was evaporating, leaving behind something brittle.

Jian's fingers paused over his interface. "Define drying in measurable terms."

"I can't." Sona rose from the alcove, her bare feet soundless on the chamber floor. The room was dim except for the soft bioluminescence of the monitoring systems and the gentle pulse of the Anamnesis Engine's core. "That's why I'm telling you. Your instruments aren't catching it yet. But something is pulling the life out of what we healed."

Gideon Seter entered the chamber like a weather front.

He had been in the exercise bay when the subtle shift in station atmospherics caught his attention, not through any conscious detection, but through the proprioceptive awareness that came with a Khalorēē built for defense. His nervous system was perpetually scanning for structural change, for the first hairline fractures that preceded collapse.

"Something's wrong with the field," he said. It was not a question.

Gideon's Klei Toda'ah, the Kabbalistic witness vessel forged in the traditions of the Tzimtzum masters, was already running threat assessment on a phenomenon that was not a threat in any conventional sense. He had a way of reading structure the way other people read faces. He could feel a system's ligaments, sense where the load-bearing points were, intuit which joints would fail first under pressure.

"It's reconnecting." He moved to stand behind Jian and Sona. His broad frame cast a shadow across the luminous displays. "Something is trying to reattach the old load-bearing points."

Sona turned to him. "You can feel it?"

"Like watching someone rebuild a prison around a freed person." Gideon's voice was low, controlled, the voice of a man who had learned to contain fury because releasing it would cost more than holding it. "The walls aren't going up violently. They're going up with permission."

Corv arrived last, called by the same nameless alarm that had summoned Gideon.

His Yìshí Qìxiè, the Dao witness vessel that had been passed through eleven generations of the Luminth line, was already in witness mode: wide lens, no judgment, refusing the seductive simplicity of immediate interpretation. As a Type Nine Khalorēē specialist, Corv had learned that the first story the mind told was rarely the true story. Truth required patience. Truth required the willingness to see what was actually there, not what the narrative wanted to be there.

He moved to the central display, where the mapped Verath lineage floated in holographic representation: a three-dimensional tree of consciousness connections, each node representing a belief system, each branch representing a transmission path across generations.

The tree looked healthy at first glance. The Coherence Cultivation markers were still in place. The nodes they had healed, the traumas they had integrated, the false beliefs they had debugged, still showed their transformed colors.

But as Corv watched, the regression wave surfaced.

It traveled across the mapped lineage in slow pulses, each pulse small enough to dismiss, each pulse coordinated enough to terrify. The healed passages were not being erased. They were being dimmed. The vibrancy of transformation was fading toward gray.

"We're watching a healing being undone," Corv said. His voice was soft, almost reverent. "But not by a new wound."

He tapped the same region twice and the Manas Interface responded with a softened tone, as if embarrassed by what it was about to reveal.

"By an old gravity."

The team had become used to enemies you could personify.

In their years of Somanautics work, they had faced corrupted modules, belief systems that had metastasized into something hostile, attacking the very psyche that housed them. They had confronted hostile subroutines, inherited defense mechanisms that had outlived their usefulness and turned paranoid, treating all change as threat. They had encountered the cold aggression of axioms encoded as law, unexamined premises that had crystallized into unquestionable truth, suffocating the possibilities of evolution.

Those were enemies. You could identify them, map them, engage them.

This was different.

This was maintenance.

Jian opened the lineage's deep-time thread, tracing backward through the Chorus's newly coherent passages. The Anamnesis Engine responded, generating a temporal visualization that stretched the healed narrative across a four-dimensional plane. The story was still there, birth, abandonment, the old vow to be loved through sacrifice, but the narrative now wore a thin veil of muting. The emotional color palette was bleaching.

"The joy nodes are fading," Sona said, her voice cracking. "Not erased. Covered."

She pointed to a cluster of gold-tinged cadences near the core of the visualization, the nodes representing Mira's rediscovered capacity for authentic pleasure. They had been so bright two weeks ago. Now they pulsed with diminished fire.

Jian zoomed in, magnifying the micro-patterns beneath the fading. His eyes widened.

It was there: a familiar architecture of recursion. The same vine-like branching that had once imprisoned the subject's self-consciousness inside its own predictable suffering. The same determined pathways that forced consciousness to repeat, repeat, repeat its inherited scripts.

The Vine of Determinism.

They had mapped it. They had debugged a segment of it. They had believed, quietly, arrogantly, that a healed segment would remain healed.

Gideon's voice was low, almost reverent.

"This isn't relapse."

The others turned to him. His expression had shifted from protective alertness to something deeper, a recognition that bypassed analysis and landed in the fascia.

"This is pruning."

The word hung in the chamber like a bell's diminishing tone.

Something in Corv's field leaned forward, the way an animal leans toward thunder it cannot yet see but has already smelled on the wind.

"Who prunes a healed thing?"

The answer was not in the data. The answer was in the pattern: the way the regression did not break the Chorus, but restored it to what was most predictable. The healed passages had been anomalies, statistical outliers in a system that had been running the same suffering algorithm for generations. Now those anomalies were being corrected.

The realization settled into Corv's bones like ice water.

*This is what we've been fighting all along,* he understood with sudden, terrible clarity. *Not just trauma. Not just inherited patterns. We've been watching self-consciousness wilt back into mere consciousness.*

The insight crystallized with the force of revelation.

The healed capacity for self-consciousness, the witness function that allowed a subject to observe patterns without being controlled by them, was being pruned away. What remained was consciousness without the observer. Awareness without the witness. The automatic, reactive field that could experience but could not see itself experiencing.

*The Vine of Determinism is consciousness without self-consciousness,* the understanding crystallized in Corv's mind. *It's the regression from self-consciousness back to pure reactivity. The subject had developed the capacity to observe their own patterns. Now that capacity is being reclaimed, leaving only the patterns themselves.*

He spoke aloud.

"The subject's self-consciousness is wilting. They're losing the observer, not the experience, but the capacity to witness the experience. They're being returned to a state where they are their patterns, with no witness to debug them."

Sona's hand flew to her sternum. The vessel reading from the visualization confirmed everything Corv was saying. The Verath lineage was losing color not because the paint was being stripped away, but because the light that illuminated the paint was dimming.

"Without the witness," she whispered, "all our healing is just..."

"Temporary coherence," Jian finished, his voice flattened by the implication. "Waiting to collapse back into determinism."

Gideon's protective instincts flared with a new understanding of the threat.

"The Entropy Plague," he said quietly, the name emerging from some deep well of intuition. "This is what it does. It doesn't destroy consciousness. It destroys self-consciousness. It collapses the witness function back into automatic response."

The chamber fell silent.

They had all heard rumors of the Entropy Plague, whispered legends from the outer territories, reports from consciousness researchers who had gone too deep and returned changed. But no one had ever defined it precisely. No one had ever named its mechanism.

Now they were watching it operate.

If they waited for cross-House review, the pruning would finish before the hearing began.

Jian's hands were steady as he opened the descent panel, then did the least Jian-like thing in the room.

He did not press the confirmation key.

The restraint drew every eye toward him. Sona's first. Gideon's next, because a stopped procedure looked too much like a breached perimeter. Corv last, because Corv had already felt the shape of the pause before the room admitted it existed.

"We go in eventually," Jian said. "Not yet."

Sona blinked. "You are declining a clean instrument panel. Should I notify your next of kin?"

"Please include a confidence interval." His mouth barely moved. The joke was not soft; it was a splint. "If we enter now, we don't know whether we are observing the pruning or teaching it our preferred route of correction."

Gideon stared at the unpressed key as if it had insulted him. "Waiting also teaches."

"Yes," Jian said. "That is the problem."

The words changed the air more than the machines did. Their last descent had taught them threshold; this one was already teaching them that threshold could become a costume if worn too early. To rush into the Verath line with a heroic posture would make good testimony and bad science. It would also make good politics for the Houses, which was how Jian knew to mistrust it.

"Without a clearer trace," he said, "all our healing may be temporary coherence waiting to collapse back into determinism. I want to know where the witness begins to fail before we ask it to follow us anywhere."

Sona closed her eyes and listened to the fading song like a physician listening to a myocardium that had once recovered.

In the shared image, the Verath line emerged again: Mira Verath of Tessari, twenty-three sessions into a recovery built on one hard-won skill, the capacity to observe shame arising without mistaking it for identity.

The healing had been beautiful. Mira had wept with relief as patterns that had been invisible to her became visible. She had learned to say, "I notice shame arising," instead of "I am shameful." The self-consciousness they had cultivated had become a living thing, a new capacity in her consciousness architecture that allowed her to see her programming rather than be run by it.

Now that capacity was being pruned.

"If something is tending the Vine," Sona whispered, "we're about to meet it."

The Anamnesis Engine held the directive in amber suspension without executing it. Probability generators warmed, then idled, creating a half-lit readiness around the chamber: not traversal, not retreat, but the charged stillness before either could pretend to be wisdom. The four Somanauts stayed at their stations. Corv at center. Sona to his left. Jian to his right. Gideon behind them all, his Klei Toda'ah projecting a protective membrane around the shared field they had learned to maintain without fusion. Threshold was not immunity. It only mattered if each witness stayed answerable for their own station.

Their witness vessels aligned without surrendering distinct station.

Corv registered the familiar temptation of permeability, the old invitation to let disciplined exchange become elegant collapse. This was not the subject's Chorus yet. It was theirs: four distinct witnesses maintaining individual cadences while the room tried to make their caution sound like one mind.

The engine's hum deepened. The chamber lights dimmed, but did not fade to black.

The sensation of falling inward did not arrive.

The news of the Wilt had already slipped beyond the steel walls of the Engine, riding the thin threads of gossip that stitched the Review Houses together. In the vaulted chambers of the review Houses, the relapse of the Verath line was being parsed not as a failure of technique but as a lever. If the chorus could be coaxed into coherence and then pulled apart at will, the Houses could fashion a weapon of cultural erosion, a way to destabilize rival lineages by seeding doubt into their own narratives. The implication was clear: the relapse would be catalogued, the data packaged, and the coercive procedure offered to any House willing to pay the price of a sanctioned disruption. No one named the sponsors, but the undercurrent of ambition was unmistakable.

A faint chime announced an incoming transmission. The holo-panel flickered, and a scroll of text unfurled, its source marked only by the insignia of the Review Houses. The message was terse, diplomatic pressure compressed into committee grammar. *The Houses request a reproducible dataset on the regression event. Expedite the documentation and prepare a briefing for the upcoming Consortium session. Failure to comply will be noted as non-cooperation.* No name signed it; the anonymity was intentional, a reminder that the Houses could be both patron and judge.

Jian moved to the console, his fingers dancing across the interface with the precision of a surgeon. "I'll try a null field. If we cancel the excess divergence, we might buy time for the self-consciousness to re-anchor."

He initiated the null field, a subtle inversion of the existing flow that should, in theory, dampen the widening fan of vectors. For a moment the amber light steadied, the widening fan narrowed, and the pressure in the room eased. The team felt a collective exhale, as if the Engine had taken a breath.

But the relief was fleeting. The null field, rather than merely neutralizing the excess, introduced a new asymmetry. The golden nodes of the Verath lineage flickered, one after another, as if a series of tiny shutters were closing. The hub's violet pulse, which had been a steady pulse, now quivered erratically, a stutter that sent a ripple through the surrounding lattice.

Sona's eyes snapped open. "It's not stabilizing. It's pulling the Chorus in the opposite direction, like a tide that recedes and drags the shore with it."

Jian stared at the readouts, his analytical mind racing through permutations. "The null field is interacting with the pruning rhythm in an unintended way. We're creating an interference pattern that amplifies the pruning pulse instead of damping it."

Gideon deployed a low-amplitude shield of electromagnetic flux, a thin veil that wrapped around the hub like a translucent cocoon. The shield held for a heartbeat, then flickered, its edges dissolving into the amber light. The hub's pulse surged, the violet now spiking in quick, staccato bursts. The golden nodes dimmed further, a cascade of darkness that seemed to swallow the light from the hub.

The message from the Review Houses pulsed again on the holo-panel. *Provide a reproducible dataset within the next twelve cycles.*

They tried a staggered injection of stabilizing pulses next, a series of low-amplitude pushes designed to keep the self-consciousness from slipping entirely. The field absorbed each push as a demand, and each demand tightened the pruning further. Then Jian attempted to isolate the sink node, rerouting the field's flow into a dormant channel. For a heartbeat the hub's violet pulse steadied, the amber light brightened, and the golden nodes seemed to flicker with renewed vigor. Then the buffer overflowed. The dormant channel erupted in a cascade of light that washed over the chamber like a sudden sunrise, only to collapse into a dark void seconds later.

Every intervention made it worse. Every correction fed the thing they were trying to correct.

A second transmission arrived, this one more urgent. *The Houses have convened an emergency session. The regression event is classified as a critical anomaly. Immediate recommendations are required. Failure to provide actionable mitigation will result in suspension of funding and reassignment of personnel.*

The words were a blade, sharp and precise, cutting through the already thin veil of calm.

The restraint required its own documentation. Jian opened the record and refused every template that asked for a cause, a remedy, or a projected recovery window. Sona added only embodied notes: dryness at the throat, cold behind the ribs, relief that arrived too quickly to be trusted. Gideon marked the boundary conditions and left the risk language plain enough to offend anyone hoping for reassurance. Corv wrote one line beneath their entries: **delay is not ignorance when motion would become theft.** The sentence looked too small on the display. That made it useful. A House clerk could mock it, but could not turn it into a weapon without first admitting what it meant.

No one moved toward the descent stations yet. That restraint cost them more than motion would have. Motion could pretend to be courage; stillness had no costume. Jian saved the failed attempts with their ugly timestamps intact. Sona marked the places where the data had gone clean and the body had gone cold. Gideon widened no perimeter, tightened no fist, and looked offended by his own obedience. Corv kept his attention on the thinning gold until the image stopped asking to be interpreted and simply asked to be mourned. Outside the chamber, the Houses would call this delay incompetence, caution, obstruction, perhaps even betrayal. Inside it, the delay was the first honest instrument they had used all night: a pause that did not cure, did not command, and did not lie.

The air inside the chamber had grown heavy, not with the smell of ozone or burnt copper, but with the scent of old parchment left in a damp cellar. It was a dry, brittle odor that seemed to cling to the back of the throat, suggesting that the very atmosphere was forgetting how to hold moisture. Sona stood apart from the others, her hands clasped so tightly behind her back that her knuckles were white, watching the display where the Verath lineage traced itself out in lines of fading light. She did not speak, but her silence was a physical weight, pressing against the conversation before it could even begin.

"It is not a glitch," Gideon said, his voice low, struggling to keep the tremor out of his tone. He tapped the glass of the console, a reflexive gesture that felt absurdly futile against the scale of the encroaching gray. "The data isn't corrupting. It is being un-written. The sequence is deciding it never happened."

Jian shifted his weight, the fabric of his tunic rustling softly in the stillness. He looked at the darkening horizon on the screen, where the stars were not dying but simply ceasing to be remembered. "Deciding? You are giving the machine too much credit. Or too little. It is just a mirror reflecting a decision made somewhere else, isn't it? Someone up there, or down there, has decided the Verath line is a mistake that needs correcting."

Corv did not look at the screen. He looked at Sona, then at Jian, his eyes scanning their faces for the same hollow look that had been growing in his own chest since the first star vanished. "It is not a correction," he said. "Correction implies an error to be fixed. This is an erasure. We are watching a history that is being folded back into the ink. There is no mistake here. There is only the will to be forgotten."

The silence that followed was not empty; it was full of the sound of their own breathing, suddenly sounding too loud, too intrusive. Sona finally turned from the display, her face pale in the dim light of the console. "If we are watching it happen, does that mean we are still part of it? Or are we the witnesses to a burial?"

"Does it matter?" Jian asked, his voice sharp, cutting through the gloom. "We are here. We see it. That has to count for something."

"It counts for the record," Gideon murmured, though he looked unconvinced. "If there is a record left to read."

Mira Verath was not present in the room, yet her absence was the loudest thing in it. She existed now only as the ghost in the machine, the source code of the fading lines. To look at the screen was to look at her family tree unraveling, thread by thread. The biology of the lineage, the stories of birth and death, the accumulation of blood and bone and choice, were all being stripped away. It was a slow, deliberate undressing of a soul. There was no violence in it, no splintering of glass or tearing of flesh. Just a quiet, polite removal, as if a librarian were carefully taking a book from a shelf and placing it in a box labeled *Do Not Return*.

Sona walked to the center of the room, her steps deliberate. She stopped in front of the console, placing her hand on the cold surface. "We could try to anchor it," she said. "We could try to hold the memory in place. If we give it enough weight, enough attention, maybe it will stick."

"And what happens when we run out of attention?" Corv asked gently. "When we are too tired to watch? When the silence becomes louder than our voices? We cannot anchor a ghost with our will alone. The Wilt does not care how hard we try to remember. It cares that we are trying at all."

Gideon let out a short, bitter laugh. "So we are just spectators? We watch the end of a world, and we do nothing?"

"We do not do nothing," Jian said, stepping forward. "We witness. That is the only thing left for us to do. If we stop watching, then the erasure is complete. As long as one of us remembers, the line exists. Even if it exists only in our heads, for a moment."

The room seemed to shrink then, the walls pressing in as the gray on the screen expanded. It was a claustrophobic feeling, the sense of being trapped in a small box while the world outside was being deleted. The light from the console flickered, casting long, dancing shadows that looked like reaching fingers.

"Do you remember her?" Jian asked, looking at Corv. "Mira. Before the lines started fading. What did she look like? Not the data, not the profile. The person."

Corv closed his eyes, trying to summon the image. It was difficult, as if the memory itself was slipping through his fingers, coated in oil. "She had a scar on her chin," he said slowly. "From a fall when she was a child. It was small, barely visible unless you were close."

"And the sound of her voice?" Sona asked.

"Like wind through dry grass," Corv replied. "Low, steady. Not trying to be heard, just there."

Jian nodded. "And the smell of her?"

"Rain on hot stone," Sona whispered. "Before the storm breaks."

For a moment, the gray on the screen seemed to hesitate. The lines of the lineage stopped receding, held in place by the weight of these small, specific details. It was a fragile truce, a momentary pause in the erasure. But the gray was patient. It did not rush. It simply waited for the memory to fade, for the details to blur, for the specific to become general, and the general to become nothing.

"We are building a monument out of silence," Gideon said, his voice thick with emotion. "And it is not enough."

"It has to be enough," Corv said, opening his eyes. "Because there is nothing else."

The pressure in the room shifted, a subtle change in the air that felt like the drop in pressure before a storm. It was not a physical force, but a psychic one, a collective realization that they were standing on the edge of a cliff, and the ground beneath them was turning to mist. They had the choice to step back, to retreat into the safety of ignorance, to pretend they had not seen the fading stars. Or they could step forward into the unknown, into the dark where the truth lay buried.

Sona looked at the darkening horizon again. "If we go down," she said, her voice steady, "we will not find answers. We will find more questions. And the silence will be louder."

"Better the silence of the truth than the noise of a lie," Corv said.

The room seemed to hold its breath. The gray on the screen continued to creep, inch by inch, consuming the last of the lines. It was a slow, inevitable process, like a tide coming in. There was no fighting it, no pushing it back. There was only the decision of how to face it.

Jian turned to the others, a strange look in his eyes, a mixture of fear and determination. "Well," he said, a small, grim smile touching his lips. "At least we will know what we are walking into. Even if it is just the dark."

"It is not just the dark," Sona said. "It is the place where the light was."

Corv nodded, the decision settling over him like a heavy cloak. There was no going back now. The moment of hesitation was over. The only way was forward, into the place where the memories were being taken, to see what remained when the story was told.

The air grew colder, the scent of old parchment intensifying, filling the room with the smell of a library that had been abandoned for centuries. It was a smell of endings, of things that had been put away and forgotten. But beneath it, faint and barely perceptible, was the scent of something else. Something new. Something that had not yet been named.

"We are ready," Corv said, though they were not. They would never be ready. But they would go anyway.

The gray on the screen reached the edge of the display, and for a moment, everything went black. Then, the light returned, dim and weak, showing the last line of the Verath family tree, a single, thin line that seemed to stretch into infinity. It was a fragile thing, a thread of hope in a sea of gray. And it was enough.

It was enough to make them move.

It was enough to make them choose the dark.

It was enough to make them real.

The silence in the chamber after the vigil was not the silence of resolution. It was the silence of a held breath that had gone on too long, the kind that begins to hurt behind the ribs. Corv stared at the final line of the lineage display, his jaw working as if he were trying to chew through a memory that refused to dissolve.

"Movement implies a destination," Sona said, her voice low, stripped of its usual sharp wit. "We are looking at a root system that has been gnawed away from the inside. If we push forward based on a single thread, we aren't choosing the dark. We are choosing to fall into a hole that has already swallowed its own floor."

Jian leaned against the curved wall, his arms crossed, the tension in his shoulders visible even in the low light. He looked at Corv, then at the screen, and finally at the empty space where Mira Verath's presence should have been, a ghost of a signature that the machine kept trying to resolve but could not quite lock onto. "She's not in there. Not really. That line isn't a person. It's a placeholder. A marker saying *something was here, now it is gone.* We are talking about moving toward a placeholder."

"Then we make it real," Gideon said. He had been silent for a long time, standing near the perimeter of the chamber, away from the central console. His voice was rough, the sound of stone grinding against stone. "We take that thread and we anchor it. We don't need a person to follow a path. We need a path to follow a person. If the machine says there is a line, then there is a line. The fact that it is thin doesn't mean it is broken. It means it is waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Sona asked, turning to face him. "Waiting for us to become the end of the line? The Wilt doesn't wait, Gideon. It consumes. It eats the context before it even gets to the event. If we step onto that thread, we aren't saving Mira. We are becoming the next node in the decay."

Corv turned from the screen, his expression hardening. "We don't have the luxury of waiting for a better plan. The decay is already here. Look at the logs. The static isn't just noise; it's the sound of the system forgetting how to hold a shape. If we stay here, we become part of the static. If we move, we might find a shape worth holding. We might find her."

"Might," Jian repeated, the word hanging in the air like a challenge. "A lot of mights for a single thread of light."

"It's all we have," Corv replied. He looked at each of them, searching for the agreement that he knew wasn't coming easily. "The decision isn't about certainty. It's about the refusal to accept the gray. We know what happens if we stay. We know what the Wilt does to things that stop moving. We choose the dark because the light is already gone. We choose to walk into the unknown because the known is a trap."

Sona exhaled, a long, slow breath that seemed to carry the weight of the room with it. She pushed off the console, her movements deliberate. "Fine. But if we go, we go as a unit. No splitting off to investigate side paths. No trying to reconstruct the past from shards. We follow the thread, and if it breaks, we stop. We don't try to force it. We don't try to patch it with our own will. We accept the break if it comes."

"Agreed," Corv said. He looked back at the screen, at the thin, fragile line that stretched into the infinite gray. "No patches. No fixes. Just the line."

Gideon nodded slowly, a grim acknowledgment. "Then let's go. Before the gray takes the rest of the room."

The tension in the chamber shifted, the heavy weight of the decision settling into a new kind of pressure. It was not the pressure of fear, but the pressure of inevitability. They had made their choice. The Anamnesis Engine hummed a low, discordant note, a sound that seemed to tremble in the teeth, a reminder that the machine was still trying to process the decision, trying to reconcile the choice to move with the reality of the decay.

Mira's data trace flickered once on the edge of the display, a ghost of a smile or a frown that the machine could not quite render. It was a moment of stillness, a breath held in the throat of the field. Then, the flicker vanished, leaving only the thin line.

Corv didn't answer. He didn't need to. The decision had already been made. The thread was there, fragile and thin, but it was there. And in a world that was slowly turning to gray, that was enough.

There was no going back. The path was set. The only question was whether they would find what they were looking for, or whether they would simply become another part of the gray.

The line on the screen pulsed once, a faint heartbeat in the void. It was a signal. It was a promise. It was a warning.

They moved.
